Pharmacy Layout Redesign

A two-semester capstone for a working pharmacy in Dearborn: measure everything, simulate the alternatives, and recommend only what the data supports.

client
CornerMed Pharmacy - Dearborn, MI
my role
Ergonomics & human factors lead (plus ethics)
team
4 industrial engineering seniors + faculty advisor
timeline
Oct 2022 - Apr 2023 · Senior Design Day, April 14, 2023
DMAICSiemens JackRULAArenaSLPEOQFMEA

the client

CornerMed Pharmacy is an independent retail pharmacy run by its owner-pharmacist and two assistants: consultations, order fulfillment, and delivery or pickup. The layout had been set up without a task analysis, which left inefficient operations and a backlog of work. The owner was our client and primary contact, and the goal was to shorten order processing and make the workspace better for the people in it.

approach

  • Define: site visits, project charter, scope (layout and in-store tasks, not delivery).
  • Measure: drew the layout to scale, measured every station and shelf height, and timed interarrivals, processing, and walking speed (56.32 in/s).
  • Analyze: observation and interviews, a SIPOC diagram, and a fishbone diagram.
  • Improve: SLP and graph-based layouts, Arena simulation, Siemens Jack posture analysis, and lean inventory planning.
  • Control: recommendations to the owner, with an FMEA for the risks of each change.

my part: ergonomics

I led the human factors work. We focused on the three most-visited stations - packing, computer, and shelving - and designed for adjustability across the population, from a 5th-percentile female to a 95th-percentile male. Before trusting Jack, I scored one real posture by hand and the same posture in Jack; the results matched closely, so Jack carried the rest of the analysis.

RULA scores upper-body posture risk from 1 (acceptable) to 7 (change immediately). Current vs. proposed, for the smallest and largest users:

station what was wrong change 5th % female 95th % male
Shelving Overreaching to the top shelves - heavy upper-arm and neck strain Three-level step stool; most-used stock moved to standing work height 7 → 3 6 → 3
Packing Upper-arm strain for shorter workers, neck strain for taller ones, standing fatigue Height-adjustable workstation and anti-fatigue mats 5 → 2 5 → 2
Computer Unsupported legs; a fixed chair strained taller users' trunks Height-adjustable chair with foot support 3 → 2 4 → 3

We wrote down the tradeoffs too, because every fix has them:

  • Adjustable stations only help if workers adjust them - they need a short training and a habit.
  • A step stool can become a tripping hazard, and it does nothing for reaching below the knee.
  • Moving inventory means relearning where things are.

Against those, the cost of mats, a stool, and adjustable chairs and desks was small next to the benefit to comfort and burnout.

team results: layout and simulation

Teammates led the facilities layout, Arena simulation, and lean and cost-benefit work; I contributed to the team's DMAIC and recommendations. We built two layout alternatives from a from-to chart of trips between stations - one with SLP, one graph-based.

The Arena model used measured distances between all 9 stations (81 pairs) with workers as transporters at the measured walking speed. Eight variants - original, SLP, and graph-based layouts, each with and without an added packing table, plus defined roles - were compared with 2-sample t-tests in Output Analyzer, and Process Analyzer tried staffing changes up to an all-pharmacist team.

The best answer was "don't move anything"

The SLP layout looked better in the raw report, but the t-tests found no statistically significant difference on any measure, and staffing changes made no difference either. The pharmacy is small enough that, at the measured walking speed, a new layout saves at most about 4 seconds. We recommended keeping the original layout - there was no justification for the cost of changing it - and put the effort where the data pointed instead.

team results: inventory

Moving the demand assumption from the third to the second standard deviation cut assumed daily demand by 10.4%. The EOQ model then moved ordering from 423 items twice a week to 185 items four to five times a week, saving 56.3% of the safety stock's holding cost.

recommendations

  • Keep the original layout - no significant gain from moving it
  • Make workstation heights adjustable, add anti-fatigue mats, provide a step stool
  • Order 185 items four to five times a week, with a 10.4% lower assumed demand
  • Repeat DMAIC at a narrower scale - order picking and packing - where the time actually goes

what it shows

  • Ergonomic analysis across the population, with the tool verified before it was trusted
  • DMAIC on a real client with real constraints
  • The discipline to recommend "no change" when the statistics say so
