the job

The Wooden Stone is a custom countertop manufacturer that bids work for general contractors. I'm on the fabrication floor every week alongside the production equipment, and I support estimating and bid preparation from product drawings, templates, and material specifications. That floor time is why the software below prices the shop the way it actually runs.

Bids used to be priced in an 8-sheet Excel template, and the rest of the office - CRM, scheduling, production, billing - ran on manual data entry. Together with the project manager I build and maintain the company's internal operations platform that replaced it, improving it from what the people using it tell us.

what I built

  • The estimating engine. I ported the pricing logic from the estimating design prototype into one pure, tested module and proved it against the prototype's own output (golden vectors) before any screen was built on it. It prices margin-on-price, so the strongest invariant in the suite is that a job with no overrides comes back at exactly the margin entered. The estimating module is checked by 1,400+ automated tests.
  • Capacity from the bottleneck. Fabrication days per trip are the slowest of three shop limits: saw load, CNC load, and the square feet of countertop the shop can turn out in a day. The resource that runs out first sets the schedule and the price.
  • Machine time, not guesses. CNC is priced on sink cutouts: each sink's count times the machine minutes for its type, in hours, against the CNC hours in a day. A sink with no type picked counts no time, and the worksheet says which rows - so a missing input is visible instead of silently guessed.
  • Trips and travel. Crews work a building floor by floor, so each floor group rounds its own trips up - one job-wide round-up was hiding two real trips. Distant jobs carry their own travel cost.
  • Landed material cost. Alternates and cut-to-size material carry the full stack - tax, duty, safeguard tariffs on engineered stone, and per-container freight - next to current material rate books.
  • From drawing to proposal. Take-off templates that price a job from its drawings (a take-off file can start an estimate), plus the proposal and quote PDFs generated from the estimate.
  • Reporting. Bid-performance reports (year-to-date bid and customer performance) and a sales scorecard tracking lead conversion, aging, review turnaround, and submission follow-ups.

engineering decisions

Quarter-day fab days

The prototype rounded fabrication days up to whole days. But the shop schedules to the quarter day, so a partial day is real work at a real cost - and rounding it up was a silent markup on small jobs that compounded on large ones. Fab days became decimal. A test pins the old round-up back on and checks the prototype's original results still match to the decimal, which proves nothing else in the math moved.

One missing flag, two different answers

The prototype's two material cost models read the "imported" flag in opposite directions: when the flag was missing, one model charged tax and the other didn't. I made the flag required on both models and at the database level, so that ambiguous state can't exist - a root-cause fix instead of a patch on whichever number looked wrong.

No rounding inside the engine

Everything stays full precision and is rounded only for display, except quantities you really buy or do in whole units - slabs, saw and CNC tables, and trips. Adding a round anywhere else silently moves money, so the rule is written at the top of the engine and the golden tests catch any drift.

infrastructure

I'm also the technical point of contact for the shop and office: I troubleshoot and repair shop equipment, servers, storage, and power protection, and I built and maintain the company's two customer-facing websites and its server, RAID 1 storage, and UPS-triggered safe-shutdown setup.

what it shows

  • Capacity planning from the real constraint, not an average
  • Cost models that match how the shop buys, cuts, and travels
  • Verification first: prove the math against the original before building on it
  • Shop-floor experience turned into tools the office uses every day
